# Coordinator discovery and usability plan

## Recruiting participants

Target 3–5 people who have screened candidates for a study at a research site in the past year. Mix at least one coordinator who works on multiple protocols and one research nurse or senior coordinator if available. A person who only searches for trials as a patient or referrer is a different user segment.

There are no warm coordinator introductions available yet. First look for public networking routes through an [ACRP chapter](https://acrpnet.org/membership/chapters-2/) and research-site or academic clinical research offices. Prepare a short invitation describing a 20-minute product-discovery conversation, the RN/BSN background, and that no patient information is requested. Send no invitations until the project owner chooses the recipients and approves the exact message. ResearchMatch is for recruiting volunteers to approved health research and is not an appropriate shortcut for recruiting coordinators to a portfolio interview.

Draft invitation for an appropriate professional contact:

> Hello — I'm an RN with a BSN building a prototype to support clinical research coordinators during preliminary screening and handoff. I'm trying to understand the actual workflow before deciding what to build. Would you be open to a 20-minute conversation about a recent screening task and the tools you use? I won't ask for patient details or confidential study materials. This is product discovery, not a clinical study or a sales call.

Do not ask for patient charts, names, dates of birth, contact information, or any other identifiable clinical material. If someone wants to show a real document, ask them to describe its structure or provide a blank/de-identified template through their approved process.

## Twenty-minute interview guide

1. “Tell me about the last person you screened for a trial. Start when they first reached your queue and walk me through the next action.”
2. “Which protocol and local documents did you consult? Where did the criteria come from?”
3. “What facts were missing? How did you record and resolve them?”
4. “Who made the formal eligibility decision? What did you hand them?”
5. “What changes to a protocol or study status force you to revisit earlier work?”
6. “Which step took the most time or produced the most rework? Show me the blank format of any checklist or handoff you use, if permitted.”

Do not show the app until after the participant describes their own process. Then ask them to complete one synthetic screen while thinking aloud. Record where they hesitate, what they mistrust, and which facts they expect to see but cannot.

## Evidence log

For each interview, capture role and site type, protocols handled, workflow stages, tools, artifacts, friction observed, direct quote with permission, and one contradiction to our assumptions. Keep interview notes separate from the public code repository unless the participant explicitly agrees to anonymized publication.

## Decision rule

Choose the first product wedge after interviews. Prefer a repeated, costly task with a clear owner and a testable outcome. If coordinators say the bottleneck is something other than prescreening or follow-up, change the build plan. Do not count a positive reaction to the interface as evidence that the workflow is valuable.

While recruitment is underway, use published coordinator-role research and public protocol examples to form hypotheses, and label them as secondary evidence. A strong public-source prototype can be built now; claims that it saves coordinators time require direct testing.

## Usability test

Use a synthetic candidate and a public study with deliberately ambiguous criteria. Ask the participant to prepare a preliminary handoff and identify what cannot yet be decided. Measure completion, missed blockers, unsupported eligibility claims, elapsed time, and confidence in the provenance of each statement. Compare against the participant's current method where feasible.
